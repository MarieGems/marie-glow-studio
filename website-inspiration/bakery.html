Bakery website design example: a $249 Starter and a $1,497 concept.

Hollis & Rye is a made-up custom cake studio. I researched real bakery sites first, wrote the words before the design, then built it twice, once like my $249 Website Starter and once like my $1,497 Complete package.

The business

Hollis & Rye

A made-up custom cake studio. Nothing here is a real client, and the photos are AI-generated.

What I built

Two levels

A one-page Starter, and a Complete site with a 3D scroll home, a cake builder, a 3D cake you design and a booking calendar with order tracking.

The research

Before any design

Five real bakery sites, read first. No search volume data.

The testing

What was checked

Accessibility checks, contrast, keyboard, 320 pixel width and reduced motion in Chrome. Not real screen readers, Safari, Firefox or phones.

Industry context

Who orders custom cakes, and what they need.

Custom cake studios are often run by the person who decorates. The people ordering are planning a birthday, a wedding or a work event, and they need three answers before they commit: what it costs, whether the date works, and what to do next.

In the five independent bakery sites I read, lead times ran from 2 to 3 days at one shop to 2 to 6 months for weddings at another, and deposits ranged from $25 or $50 to a $100 non-refundable deposit to full payment at order. Those are single data points from each bakery’s own pages, not an industry standard.

The customer problem

Days of emails before anyone names a price.

Across those five sites, here is what I found when I looked at the first screen and the ordering path:

  • None of the five put a quote or order button on the first screen.
  • Three of the five publish no price for custom cakes and send you to a quote form.
  • One navigation had 14 links, so the quote link was one of many. On a phone, another site’s logo was cut off at the edge.
  • None showed whether a date was workable before you wrote to them.

That became the brief for the demo: put the button on the first screen, say the price, and answer the date question honestly.

Research

What I looked at, and what I could not.

I analyzed five real bakery sites for messaging, navigation, calls to action, ordering flow and trust signals, and read search results for the questions customers ask. The first pass used a text-only tool that could not judge typography or photography, so I took real browser screenshots of all five at desktop and phone size.

Limits: I did not use keyword volume data, so there are no search volume claims here. Two sites could not be reviewed (one returned an error and one loaded only its navigation), and the customer objections are inferred from bakery policy pages, not from interviews.

One page, one promise, one button.

The copy came first, in a fixed order: promise, problem, proof, button, bridge, ask, button, with the same button words both times (“Request a quote”). The proof is a sample quote with made-up prices that says so, because there are no real reviews to quote.

Not included: e-commerce, custom photography or copywriting, unlimited revisions. In this demo the quote button goes to a placeholder page that sends nothing.

Interaction

Features that answer the customer’s questions.

  • Estimate as you choose: the price updates with each choice, and the math is checked ($96 for an 8 inch, $239 for a two tier with extras and delivery).
  • Is my date workable? A date checker compares your date to the lead-time table. It never says a date is open, because a person has to confirm that.
  • Shareable build: your choices live in the page address, so you can copy the link or carry them to the request page.
  • 3D scroll home (included): a cake built from plain shapes flies down the page, changes its finish at each step and keeps clear of the text where it stops.
  • 3D cake with booking (included): a cake you turn by dragging or with the arrow keys, with a demo calendar and a deposit screen that has no card fields.

Why it looks the way it does.

Words before design

The approved copy decided the layout, so the page reads promise, problem, proof, ask.

Three directions, one pick

I compared an editorial, a bold and a minimal direction and chose the editorial layout with the bold direction’s button. I also caught myself writing headlines that were not in the approved copy and put the approved words back.

The background changed after a design checker flagged it

A warm cream is the default “tasteful” look, so I tried blush, apricot, sage and lilac. Sage passed and goes with the terracotta button.

Contrast rules came from measuring

The button text is white on terracotta at 4.95 to 1, which passes with little room. Terracotta as text on the sage background fails, so it is a button fill only.

Motion has a job

The sample quote lines appear one by one like a receipt printing, because the promise is the price in writing. Everything stops for visitors who ask for reduced motion.

Code highlights

The receipt reveal, in plain code.

The quote lines are visible by default. A script marks the card once it scrolls into view, and the style only hides the lines when motion is allowed and JavaScript is on, so nothing can stay invisible.

One known limit: a tool that captures the page without scrolling shows an empty card there. The notes in the project say so, and printing forces the lines visible.

Lessons

What I would tell the next client.

  • Write the words first. The design gets easier when the order of the page is already decided.
  • Put the price and the next step where people can see them. In my research none of the five sites put a quote button on the first screen.
  • Let the checkers argue with you, then measure. A font and a background changed after a design checker flagged them, and a keyboard check found a footer focus ring that was brown on brown.
  • Label what is a demo and what is custom. The scroll-driven 3D homepage, the cake builder, the 3D cake design and the booking calendar with order tracking are all part of the Complete package, and saying so is part of the work.
  • Test the boring things: a phone, the keyboard, reduced motion and JavaScript off.

What was tested, and what was not

Tested in Chrome: automated accessibility checks (0 violations at the end, on every page at desktop and phone size), text contrast on every text style, keyboard use, 320 pixel width, text spacing, reduced motion and JavaScript off. Not tested: real screen readers, Safari, Firefox, real phones and slow connections. This is not a certification of accessibility.
